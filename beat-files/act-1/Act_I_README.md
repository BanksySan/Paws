# Act I — README

## Purpose

Act I establishes Cat and Dog as two competent animals living in the same household but experiencing fundamentally different worlds.

The act should allow the reader to understand both animals **before either animal understands the other**.

The central dramatic job is to establish that their conflicts do not arise because one is sensible and the other foolish. They arise because each:

- receives different information;
- inhabits a different body;
- faces different risks;
- uses different strategies;
- and assumes the other is experiencing roughly the same world.

The reader should begin to see the misunderstanding before Cat and Dog do.

---

## Starting state

Cat and Dog already share a home and a family.

They are familiar with one another, but familiarity has not produced understanding.

Each has developed a set of strategies that work extremely well in their own body and sensory world.

### Cat's normal operating assumptions

Cat tends to gather information before committing herself.

Her world is strongly shaped by:

- sound;
- movement;
- spatial geometry;
- height;
- cover;
- escape routes;
- controlled distance;
- warmth and dryness.

She is not timid. Her behaviour is adapted to being small, light, agile and comparatively vulnerable to larger bodies.

### Dog's normal operating assumptions

Dog tends to gain information by engaging with the world.

His world is strongly shaped by:

- smell;
- proximity;
- movement;
- social interaction;
- physical robustness;
- shared activity;
- familiar humans;
- direct investigation.

He is not reckless. His body and experience have repeatedly taught him that moving towards things usually works.

---

## Act progression

The act should broadly move through:

**competence → contrast → contact → misunderstanding**

First, each animal is allowed to make complete sense on their own terms.

Then those two systems are placed together.

The same behaviour that is reasonable from one point of view becomes baffling from the other.

By the end of the act, both should have reasons to believe that the other behaves irrationally.

The reader should know better.

---

## Core jobs of the act

Act I should establish:

- Cat's outdoor world of routes, listening, stillness, height and controlled risk.
- Dog's outdoor world of scent, movement, social encounters and physical confidence.
- Cat's relationship with cold, snow, wetness and warmth.
- Dog's relative indifference to ordinary cold, snow, puddles and mud while active.
- Dog's deep pleasure in being with his humans.
- Cat's genuine affection for her humans, expressed differently.
- Dog's physical robustness and willingness to recover quickly from minor falls and collisions.
- Cat's use of stillness as active investigation.
- Dog's use of proximity as active investigation.
- Cat's escalating distance-making signals.
- Dog's tendency to continue an interaction he believes is unfinished.
- The tail-language misunderstanding.
- Height as a normal route and safety mechanism for Cat.
- Height as mostly unavailable territory for Dog.
- The fact that both animals are competent in their own bodies.
- The household routines that the body swap will later disrupt.

---

## Important recurring setup

### Distance

Cat often gains information by **increasing or preserving distance**.

Dog often gains information by **reducing distance**.

This should create conflict naturally.

### The tail

Cat's stress can be shown through an escalation such as:

**barely perceptible movement → twitch → flick → lash**

Dog may interpret visible tail movement through his own social experience and fail to recognise the warning.

### Stillness

Cat appearing to do nothing should repeatedly be shown to involve active listening, watching and assessment.

### Physical confidence

Dog's confidence should be grounded partly in experience.

He can:

- run hard;
- fall;
- collide;
- wrestle;
- get wet;
- get muddy;
- recover;
- continue.

He does not need to think of himself as robust. His body has simply taught him that many physical mistakes are survivable.

### Humans

Both animals should love the humans.

Their relationships should not be ranked.

Dog's affection often appears through:

- proximity;
- shared activity;
- following;
- greeting;
- touch;
- responding to praise.

Cat's affection may appear through:

- choosing trusted company;
- returning home;
- accepting contact from familiar humans;
- rubbing;
- scent sharing;
- purring;
- resting near them.

---

## Current opening structure

The current material includes:

### Chapter 1 — Cat begins her evening patrol

Cat's world is established on its own terms.

### Chapter 2 — Dog goes for his evening walk

Dog's world is established on its own terms.

### Chapter 3 — The Towel Escape — Dog POV

Dog attempts a friendly greeting and experiences Cat's defensive response as inexplicable hostility.

### Chapter 4 — Cat on the Bed — Cat POV

The same physical event is experienced as a sequence of retreat, lost escape options, ignored warnings and finally necessary distance-making.

These repeated POV chapters are important because they demonstrate the book's central idea before the body swap occurs.

---

## End state

By the end of Act I:

- Cat and Dog should both feel that the other behaves strangely.
- Neither should yet understand why.
- Their misunderstandings should feel accumulated rather than arbitrary.
- The reader should already be capable of understanding both sides.
- Each animal's normal body and senses should feel familiar enough that losing them will matter.

The desired emotional position is not:

> I hate you.

It is closer to:

> Why are you always like this?

---

## Guardrails

### Do not make personality a list of human labels

Avoid relying on labels such as:

- proud;
- vain;
- dignified;
- gullible;
- generous;
- cowardly;
- brave.

Personality should emerge from repeated behavioural choices, thresholds, habits and preferences.

### Do not make Cat an anti-Dog

Cat is not defined by disliking everything Dog likes.

### Do not make Dog a foolish Cat

Dog's directness should remain rational within his body and sensory world.

### Do not make either worldview morally superior

The act should not quietly teach that caution is better than enthusiasm, or vice versa.

### Do not over-explain

The reader should infer the misunderstanding from action whenever possible.

---

## Author questions to keep asking

For every substantial scene:

- What information does Cat have that Dog does not?
- What information does Dog have that Cat does not?
- What can one physically do that the other cannot?
- What risk matters more to one than the other?
- What does each assume the other already understands?
- What will the reader understand that the POV character does not?

---

## Act I north star

**Cat and Dog each make complete sense when experienced from the inside.**

**The problem begins when each assumes the other lives in the same world.**
