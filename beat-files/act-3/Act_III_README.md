# Act III — README

## Purpose

Act III turns disorientation into discovery.

Cat and Dog begin learning how the other's body and senses actually work.

The important revelation is not that the new body is better.

It is that the new body solves different problems.

The governing progression is:

**experiment → discovery → reluctant question → instruction → success → respect → cooperation**

---

## Starting state

At the beginning of Act III:

- Cat is still Cat mentally, inside Dog's body.
- Dog is still Dog mentally, inside Cat's body.
- both remain frustrated;
- both have failed using familiar strategies;
- both have encountered evidence that the other's behaviour may not be arbitrary.

Neither is yet skilled in the other's body.

---

## Core dramatic principle

Act III should complete the progression:

> This body is terrible.

to:

> I have been using it wrongly.

and then towards:

> You know this body. Tell me what it can do.

The growing relationship should become practical before it becomes sentimental.

They should need one another's expertise.

---

## Core jobs of the act

Act III should allow Cat to discover:

- smell as history rather than mere odour;
- smell as direction;
- why proximity can be necessary for information;
- the physical pleasure of running, chasing and retrieving;
- Dog's shared ball ritual with Human;
- strength as a useful option;
- robustness as a source of physical confidence;
- that direct action can sometimes solve a problem more efficiently than perfect positioning.

Act III should allow Dog to discover:

- height as route and safety;
- gaps and indirect paths as practical solutions;
- stillness as active investigation;
- detailed hearing as a way of gathering information at distance;
- why escape can be rational rather than cowardly;
- why being small changes what an approaching body feels like;
- that Cat's warnings often come after easier methods of creating distance have failed.

---

## Suggested discovery sequence

### Cat — Dog's nose

Cat deliberately uses smell rather than merely enduring it.

A missing or absent thing can still leave useful information behind.

The world gains a history.

### Dog — Cat's routes

Dog learns that obstacles do not always need to be pushed through.

The environment becomes three-dimensional.

A fence becomes a road.

### Cat — The ball

Cat experiences:

- acceleration;
- chase;
- carrying;
- returning;
- Human's participation.

She does not need to become ball-obsessed.

She only needs to understand why the ritual makes sense from inside Dog's body.

### Dog — Stillness

Dog is required not to approach.

He waits.

Detailed sound gradually reveals information.

He discovers that doing nothing visibly can still be active investigation.

### Cat — Strength

Cat encounters an obstacle and searches for an elegant route.

Dog's answer is simpler:

> Push it.

The crude solution works.

Cat discovers that physical force can be rational.

### Dog — Escape is not failure

Dog faces an ordinary threat that would be manageable in his old body but unwise to confront in Cat's.

Cat tells him to leave.

He does.

The problem passes without confrontation.

Dog learns that safety can come from not being present when danger arrives.

---

## Teaching and dialogue

Act III should increasingly allow Cat and Dog to coach one another.

Useful exchanges may take forms such as:

> Stop looking. Smell.

> Don't push it. Go up.

> Wait.

> Don't wait. Run.

> You can make that jump.

> Push harder.

The point is not that one becomes the other's instructor permanently.

Expertise should alternate according to body and situation.

---

## Behavioural discovery rather than moral conversion

Cat should not learn:

> Dog is brave and I should be braver.

She should learn:

> Dog's size, strength, scent and robustness make direct action sensible in situations where it would not be sensible for me.

Dog should not learn:

> Cat is cautious and I should be more cautious.

He should learn:

> Cat's small body, hearing, routes and escape options make distance and early avoidance sensible in situations where I would normally approach.

Neither animal should become more like the other.

---

## Echoes of Act I

Act III can deliberately revisit selected Act I mechanics.

Possible echoes include:

- Dog-in-Cat facing an open door and finally understanding Cat's pause.
- Cat-in-Dog discovering why a fall or collision is less frightening in Dog's body.
- Dog-in-Cat using a fence or tree as part of a route.
- Cat-in-Dog finding something through smell as Dog found the lost ball.
- Dog-in-Cat discovering stillness as Cat did during the hunt.
- Cat-in-Dog experiencing the ball chase from inside Dog.
- Dog-in-Cat being approached by something large.
- Cat-in-Dog discovering that weight and strength can alter the environment.

These echoes should feel like recognition, not repetition.

Not every Act I moment needs a mirrored counterpart.

---

## Small cooperative success

Before the major jeopardy, Cat and Dog should solve one smaller problem together.

The problem should require complementary information or abilities.

One may know:

- where;
- when;
- what moved;
- who passed;
- what is behind something.

The other may be able to:

- reach;
- push;
- follow;
- climb;
- fit through;
- track.

The important change is in the questions they ask.

Earlier:

> You're doing it wrong.

Now:

> What can you hear?

> What can you smell?

> Can you reach that?

> Can you move this?

The solution should prove that cooperation is practically useful before the story asks them to depend on it under genuine danger.

---

## End state

By the end of Act III:

- Cat and Dog understand several of the reasons behind the other's behaviour.
- Each has experienced abilities they previously dismissed or misunderstood.
- Each has experienced vulnerabilities they previously failed to appreciate.
- They have begun asking for and trusting the other's expertise.
- Cooperation has become possible.
- Their differences remain intact.

The emotional progression should be visible through behaviour rather than speeches.

---

## Structural decision deliberately left open

The timing of the return to their original bodies remains open.

Two versions remain viable.

### Still swapped entering the main jeopardy

The climax tests trust in the other's expertise.

The governing idea becomes:

> You know this body. Tell me what it can do.

### Returned before the main jeopardy

The climax tests whether they can apply what they learned while fully themselves.

The governing idea becomes:

> You know something I don't. What do you know?

Act III should preserve both options until the later structure makes one clearly stronger.

---

## Guardrails

### Do not make discovery symmetrical for its own sake

Not every Cat lesson needs an equal-and-opposite Dog lesson.

### Do not erase frustration

Learning should involve mistakes, awkwardness and occasional disagreement.

### Do not make one body secretly superior

Each body should reveal capabilities and limitations.

### Do not turn coaching into exposition

Instruction should arise because something practical needs to happen.

### Do not complete the emotional arc too early

Respect and cooperation can grow here, but the later jeopardy still needs to test them.

---

## Author questions to keep asking

- What is the POV character attempting?
- What would they have done in their original body?
- What can this body do instead?
- Who knows that?
- Does the POV character ask for help willingly or reluctantly?
- What new information becomes available?
- What old assumption does the discovery undermine?
- How can this discovery later matter under jeopardy?

---

## Act III north star

**Cat and Dog stop asking why the other's body cannot behave like their own.**

**They begin discovering what the other's body can do that theirs cannot.**
