# Character File: Dog

## Purpose of this file

This is an **author-facing character bible** for Dog: a guide to his sensory world, physical experience, social instincts and emotional logic, designed to keep his point of view consistent before and after the body swap.

The governing idea is:

> **Dog does not behave like a noisy, clumsy cat. His behaviour makes sense because proximity gives him information, his body makes physical contact less threatening, and much of his world arrives through smell.**

Dog is an **English Springer Spaniel**: a sturdy, active, sociable family dog with substantial physical momentum, a strong interest in scent, a love of movement and retrieving, and little concern for ordinary cold, rain or mud when something interesting is happening.

The breed sharpens Dog's physical and behavioural possibilities; it does not determine his personality. He is this particular Springer, not a catalogue of breed stereotypes.

---

# 1. Core character idea

Dog is **social, physical, curious, optimistic and inclined to engage**.

Where Cat tries to understand the situation before entering it, Dog often understands the situation **by entering it**.

His basic strategy is:

**Notice → approach → sniff/engage → understand → respond.**

That distinction is crucial.

Cat can gather excellent information from a distance using hearing and vision.

Dog's greatest sensory gift often becomes richer as he gets nearer.

So when Dog approaches something uncertain, he is not necessarily being careless.

He is trying to find out what it is.

---

# 2. Dog's sensory world

## Sensory hierarchy

For the story, Dog's world can be written primarily through:

1. **Smell** — identity, history, direction, emotion, presence.
2. **Hearing** — alerts, voices, movement, distant events.
3. **Body and touch** — contact, pressure, movement, physical play.
4. **Sight** — important, especially socially, but less information-rich than it is for a human.
5. **Taste** — closely linked with his tendency to investigate physically.

Dog is not visually incompetent. He reads posture, gestures and movement extremely well. But smell offers him a kind of information that humans — and even Cat — cannot fully share.

---

# 3. Smell: Dog's other dimension

Human beings tend to imagine smell as an adjective:

**The tree smells interesting.**

Dog's experience is more usefully imagined as:

**The smell contains information.**

A place is layered with traces of what has happened there.

The pavement does not merely have a smell. It contains:

- the dog that passed earlier;
- the direction it travelled;
- where it paused;
- urine marks from several animals;
- food dropped by a child;
- fox or cat traces;
- damp soil;
- a person who stood there;
- the changing effect of rain, wind and time.

Dog moves through a partially invisible **information landscape**.

### Writing consequence

Dog's descriptions need not be endlessly olfactory or full of invented perfume vocabulary.

Instead, smell can alter **attention and causality**.

Dog suddenly pulls towards a hedge because something happened there.

He pauses at a lamppost because it contains news.

He knows somebody has been in the house before the humans notice anything visibly disturbed.

He can follow an individual who is no longer present.

Smell allows the past to remain physically relevant.

That is extraordinary from a human or feline point of view.

---

## Smell requires proximity

This is one of the book's central mechanics.

Vision and hearing can gather rich information from far away.

Smell can travel at distance, but detailed investigation often improves when Dog **gets close**.

So Dog's instincts repeatedly tell him:

**Go over there.**

**Put your nose near it.**

**Find out.**

Cat sees an unknown dog and gathers information while remaining on the wall.

Dog sees an unknown dog and wants to approach until the other animal becomes a richly informative individual rather than a distant outline.

Cat interprets this as reckless sociability.

Dog experiences it as research.

---

# 4. Hearing

Dog hears substantially better than a human, especially at higher frequencies, and can notice distant or quiet events humans miss.

But in the narrative, hearing can play a different role for Dog than for Cat.

Cat may use sound to **localise and analyse** tiny events.

Dog may use sound more often to **alert and orient**:

- a familiar car;
- footsteps approaching the house;
- the click of a lead clasp;
- another dog barking streets away;
- somebody at the door;
- the rustle associated with food;
- a child crying in another room.

Then his nose and body become involved.

Sound says:

**Something is happening.**

Smell and approach say:

**Here is what it is.**

---

# 5. Sight

Dog uses vision effectively for:

- body language;
- human gestures;
- movement;
- recognising familiar beings and places;
- coordinating social play;
- anticipating thrown objects;
- following where a human points or looks.

Dogs are particularly good at living with humans partly because they are unusually attentive to our behaviour.

So Dog's visual world should contain **social meaning**.

He watches faces, hands, posture, doors, coats, shoes and familiar routines.

A human merely reaching towards the cupboard may predict food.

Somebody putting on particular shoes may predict a walk.

The child grabbing a ball may transform the entire emotional meaning of the room. For this Dog, a thrown ball is not a minor amusement but one of the clearest invitations to shared action.

---

# 6. Dog's body

## Size

As a sturdy adult English Springer Spaniel, Dog's size and weight give him a form of background security he may never consciously recognise until he loses it.

Most household objects are not threats.

A human walking towards him is not an approaching wall of mass in the way it can be for Cat.

He can absorb bumps and collisions that would matter more to her.

He can push through vegetation.

He can jump down from things without first calculating whether someone might land on him.

He does not need an escape route through a three-inch gap because very little in his normal family life requires one.

### Narrative implication

Dog's optimism is not purely personality.

The world has repeatedly taught him that **approaching things usually works out**.

---

## Strength

Dog can:

- pull;
- shove;
- brace;
- knock things over accidentally;
- carry and retrieve objects;
- absorb impact;
- shoulder through undergrowth;
- occupy a doorway simply by standing in it;
- bowl into legs, furniture or people through enthusiasm rather than aggression.

He may have little awareness of how intimidating this looks to Cat.

If he puts one paw beside her during an enthusiastic attempt to play, he may experience himself as friendly.

Cat sees a limb almost the size of her chest striking the floor beside her.

Both perceptions are true.

---

## Momentum

Dog's body is less instantly redirectable than Cat's.

Once committed, he takes space to stop or turn. An enthusiastic Springer at speed has real momentum, even when Dog himself is barely conscious of being powerful.

This can make him seem clumsy from Cat's perspective.

But momentum has advantages:

- pushing through;
- covering ground;
- using body weight;
- sustained running;
- physical intervention.

During jeopardy, Dog's body should be useful precisely because it is **not** a cat's body.

---

# 7. Movement

Dog's characteristic rhythm can be:

**forward, forward, pause-to-sniff, veer towards something interesting, forward again.**

A thrown ball can interrupt almost any sequence with a different but equally characteristic rhythm:

**see movement → chase → seize → retrieve → ready for it to happen again.**

Compared with Cat, his movement is:

- more continuous;
- more grounded;
- less vertical;
- more physical;
- more likely to involve contact;
- more likely to announce itself.

He does not naturally think in terms of perches and escape gaps.

The floor is where the world happens.

A sofa may be for sleeping rather than reconnaissance.

A fence is a barrier, not a roadway.

A tree is principally a magnificent collection of smells rather than a possible route upwards.

---

# 8. Dog outdoors

Dog's walk is one of the richest periods of his day.

Humans often understand the walk as exercise plus destination:

**We walked around the park and came home.**

Dog can experience the same journey as a flood of social and environmental information.

He may:

- leave the front door already smelling what happened nearby;
- quarter or weave across interesting ground rather than treating the path as the only route;
- check the same lamppost he checked yesterday because its information has changed;
- find the route taken by another dog;
- recognise familiar neighbourhood dogs before seeing them;
- investigate food traces;
- smell wildlife;
- learn that a human or animal crossed the path earlier;
- greet known dogs;
- examine unfamiliar dogs;
- plunge into grass, undergrowth, puddles or mud because scent or movement leads there;
- abandon almost everything for a thrown ball, then return it in expectation that the excellent event will happen again;
- become fascinated by something the human cannot perceive at all.

This means repetition does not make the walk repetitive.

To the human it is the same street.

To Dog it has been rewritten since yesterday.

---

# 9. Other dogs

Dog is socially interested, but avoid the old simplified idea that dogs experience every interaction through a rigid "pack hierarchy".

Domestic dog social structure is flexible. Relationships depend on history, context, motivation and individual temperament.

Dog may have:

- particular friends;
- dogs he finds exciting;
- dogs he avoids;
- dogs whose play style matches his;
- dogs he recognises only by scent until they come into view;
- familiar social rituals.

### A useful opening-walk mechanism

Dog can meet several doggy acquaintances without the encounters becoming exposition.

Each greeting demonstrates how Dog gathers information:

**approach, sniff, circle, posture, movement, play invitation, separation.**

To Dog this is normal social literacy.

To Cat, observing from a wall, it may look like several large animals rushing directly into one another's personal space and sticking their noses into outrageous places.

---

# 10. Humans

Humans are central to Dog's social world.

Dog is unusually attentive to human cues and routines.

He knows:

- which footsteps belong to whom;
- which voice means food;
- which voice means trouble;
- the difference between school-morning activity and weekend activity;
- which coat predicts a walk;
- which drawer contains the lead;
- where each family member tends to sit;
- the emotional difference between being called and being *Called*.

He may not understand human language as humans do, but he lives inside a dense network of repeated associations, tone, posture, scent and routine.

### Emotional consequence

Dog may regard proximity to the family as a reward in itself.

Being excluded from a room can therefore feel very different to him than it does to Cat.

Cat may voluntarily leave to be alone.

Dog may wonder why anybody would voluntarily leave the people when the people are right there.

---

# 11. Sociality and friendliness

Dog's default optimism can be:

**This may be a friend until I learn otherwise.**

Cat's may be closer to:

**This is unknown until I learn otherwise.**

Neither is universally true of real cats or dogs, but it is a powerful character contrast.

Dog's sociability gives him strengths:

- he recruits help;
- approaches humans;
- communicates loudly;
- works readily beside others;
- tolerates close physical presence;
- forms rapid positive associations.

It also gives him weaknesses:

- he may assume friendliness where caution is warranted;
- he may rush into situations;
- he may overwhelm Cat;
- he may be emotionally shocked when a human behaves violently towards him.

That last point is especially important for the burglary climax.

Dog's confidence around people can become a vulnerability precisely because it has usually been justified.

---

# 12. Communication

Dog communicates through:

- tail position and motion;
- whole-body posture;
- facial tension;
- ears;
- mouth;
- gaze;
- vocalisation;
- movement;
- touch;
- smell.

## Tail wagging

A wag does not simply mean "happy" in every circumstance, but Dog associates tail movement with social arousal and communication.

Therefore Cat's lashing or twitching tail is easy for him to misread.

He sees movement and engagement.

She is signalling irritation.

The mistake is not foolish. It is **cross-species translation failure**.

---

## Barking

Dog's bark can serve many functions:

- alert;
- invitation;
- excitement;
- frustration;
- alarm;
- defence;
- social contact.

To Dog, barking is information and action.

To Cat, standing a few feet away, it may also be an overwhelming physical event: sudden, loud and produced by a much larger animal.

Dog may therefore think:

**I was only telling her!**

Cat thinks:

**He exploded. Again.**

---

# 13. Touch

Dog may be comfortable with a level of physical closeness that Cat finds intrusive.

He may:

- lean;
- bump;
- nose;
- paw;
- lie against;
- wrestle;
- chase;
- press into a human's legs.

Touch can be social and reassuring.

Again, size changes the meaning.

Dog's affectionate nudge can physically move Cat.

He may never realise this until he is living inside Cat's body and receives one of his own gestures from the outside.

---

# 14. Weather

## Rain

Dog is unusually untroubled by ordinary rain.

He is not literally weatherproof, and extreme cold or prolonged exposure still matter, but this particular active, robust Springer rarely regards wet weather as a sufficient reason to stop doing something interesting.

His coat, size, activity and temperament make rain less disruptive than it is for Cat.

More importantly, wet weather changes Dog's **smell-world**.

Moisture, disturbed soil, moving air and wet vegetation can alter how scents are carried and perceived.

So while Cat may be thinking about soaked fur, wet paws and the nearest dry route, Dog may already have forgotten that it is raining because something fascinating has happened beside the hedge.

Puddles and mud are obstacles only in the loosest possible sense.

---

## Cold

Dog almost never feels ordinary cool weather as Cat does.

He is active, well coated and usually busy.

Movement generates warmth, and a Springer walk tends to contain quite a lot of movement.

Dog on a walk is rarely trying to find a sunbeam.

He is doing things.

That creates an important experiential contrast:

**Cat seeks favourable conditions. Dog often generates comfort through activity.**

This should remain character-specific rather than becoming a universal claim that dogs do not feel cold.

---

## Snow

Snow can be sensory novelty:

- altered smells;
- buried traces;
- cold air;
- new textures;
- moving flakes;
- a landscape physically changed overnight.

Dog may plunge into it because novelty promises information, movement and play.

Cat may look at him as though he has volunteered to jump into a bath.

---

# 15. Food

Food is not only taste.

Dog's nose often announces food long before anybody serves it.

He may know:

- who has eaten;
- which cupboard was opened;
- what is in a shopping bag;
- that food fell beneath the table several minutes ago;
- that a child's hand still carries traces of lunch.

His apparent obsession with food can therefore be partly sensory: food is broadcasting itself at him.

Cat may find Dog's inability to ignore it lacking in dignity.

After the swap she may discover that Dog has been living inside an advertisement she could barely perceive.

---

# 16. Danger

Dog's danger response can differ dramatically from Cat's because his body and social strategies differ.

A plausible sequence for this Dog is:

**notice → approach or stand ground → get larger/louder → recruit humans → bark → physically intervene if necessary.**

This does not mean he is fearless or aggressive.

His size and bark make display itself useful.

Cat's primary safety may come from being absent when danger arrives.

Dog's may come from making the danger reconsider.

---

## The danger of misplaced trust

Because Dog is friendly and expects humans to be safe, deliberate human violence can momentarily break his model of the world.

If a burglar strikes or kicks the body Dog normally inhabits, the first response associated with that body and history need not be instant heroic attack.

It may be:

**shock.**

This person is behaving impossibly.

That vulnerability remains important whichever version of the climax we ultimately choose.

### If the burglary happens after the return

Dog can be frightened or hurt in his own body. Cat's intervention matters because she chooses to defend him using her own feline strengths. Dog then recovers and uses the abilities of his own body and voice: barking, movement, size, persistence and obstruction.

### If the burglary happens while they are still swapped

Cat may be the one occupying Dog's body when human aggression arrives. Each retains their own complete non-physical persona, including memories, wishes and fears. Dog's history of trusting humans belongs to him, rather than transferring to Cat with his body. Both must use what they have learned about their unfamiliar bodies and each other's behaviour; without a shared language, they cannot verbally coach one another.

The emotional function is similar in either version:

**trust replaces assumption.**

Neither one should "win" alone.

---

# 17. Emotional logic

Dog's deepest needs may include:

- **social connection**;
- **participation**;
- **information through investigation**;
- **movement**;
- **predictable human relationships**;
- **shared activity**;
- **belonging**.

His strongest negative experiences may include:

- exclusion;
- inability to reach someone he cares about;
- confusing hostility;
- being prevented from investigating something important;
- separation from the family;
- discovering that friendliness is not reciprocated.

His arc is **not** learning to stop being enthusiastic.

It is learning that enthusiasm without attention can become threatening to someone smaller and differently equipped.

---

# 18. Dog's view of Cat before the swap

Cat is:

- unnecessarily complicated;
- difficult to persuade into games;
- always disappearing somewhere inaccessible;
- strangely offended by perfectly good smells;
- unwilling to come when exciting things happen;
- capable of sitting silently for absurd lengths of time;
- inexplicably furious when her tail is clearly moving;
- rather pointy at the ends;
- unfairly allowed on furniture he cannot reach;
- sometimes warm and pleasant when she forgets to be difficult;
- probably fond of him, though peculiarly determined not to show it.

Dog's frustration is affectionate rather than contemptuous.

He genuinely wants Cat to join in.

That makes his intrusion more poignant: **he is often annoying her while trying to include her**.

---

# 19. Dog's hidden misunderstanding

Dog assumes Cat experiences roughly the same world he does but responds differently by choice.

Therefore:

- if she runs away, perhaps she wants chasing;
- if she sits high up, perhaps she is refusing to join in;
- if she does not approach, perhaps she is unfriendly;
- if she hates rain, perhaps she is fussy;
- if she hisses after he follows her, perhaps her mood changed suddenly;
- if she hides from something he considers harmless, perhaps she is timid.

The body swap reveals the error:

**Cat is not applying bad rules to Dog's world. She lives in a different world.**

---

# 20. Dog inside Cat's body

Dog's experience of the swap should initially be frightening in ways Cat's is exhilarating.

## First shocks

### Smallness

The world becomes enormous.

Human feet matter.

Doors move frighteningly fast.

The gap beneath furniture suddenly looks useful.

Dog's old Springer body, occupied by Cat, is gigantic beside the small tortoiseshell body he now inhabits.

He finally sees himself from below.

That alone may transform his understanding of Cat.

### Lack of strength

Dog tries to solve a problem by pushing it.

Nothing happens.

He tries harder.

Still nothing.

The obvious solution — force — is gone.

Then he discovers Cat's alternatives:

**around, beneath, above, through.**

He learns that agility is not a lesser form of strength. It is a different answer to the physical world.

### Hearing

Tiny sounds suddenly have location and urgency.

The house is not quiet.

The walls tick.

Something moves beneath the floorboards.

A bird lands outside.

A child's fingers brush a duvet in the next room.

Dog may find it almost impossible to relax at first because **everything is happening**.

Then he learns which sounds matter.

### Low light

The dark becomes navigable.

Places he once regarded as visually empty contain edges and routes.

He understands why Cat can move through the house at night without announcing herself.

### Whiskers

Close spaces seem to communicate directly with his face.

He discovers that Cat's celebrated ability to squeeze and navigate is not reckless luck. Her body is supplying information he never possessed.

### Being approached

This is the great emotional lesson.

A happy large dog comes bounding towards Dog-in-Cat. It may even move with the same sort of loose, friendly confidence Dog once considered obviously harmless.

Dog knows intellectually that the animal is friendly.

His new body may disagree.

The noise is huge.

The paws hit the ground hard.

The mouth is enormous.

The momentum is terrifying.

Suddenly Cat's reactions stop looking theatrical.

They feel sensible.

---

# 21. Dog's post-swap growth

Dog does not become cat-like.

He retains:

- enthusiasm;
- friendliness;
- love of investigation;
- willingness to act;
- physical playfulness;
- desire to be involved;
- delight in chasing and retrieving, especially balls.

But he now understands:

- that approaching is itself pressure;
- that a small body cannot assume large bodies will stop safely;
- that retreat may be intelligent rather than fearful;
- that distance is a form of communication;
- that Cat has been signalling long before she hisses;
- that quiet observation can discover things he misses;
- that being high up can feel secure rather than antisocial;
- that rain is materially different when it soaks the body you depend on for speed and insulation.

After returning to himself, his most meaningful act may be astonishingly small:

**Cat withdraws, and Dog does not follow.**

That may say more about his transformation than a speech ever could.

---

# 22. Voice guide for Dog POV

Dog's prose should tend towards:

- movement;
- association;
- immediacy;
- social interpretation;
- scent-triggered diversion;
- optimism;
- physical verbs;
- comic certainty;
- sudden enthusiasm.

He does not need to narrate smells as elaborate human metaphors.

Instead, scents can produce knowledge or action.

### Useful rhythm

**Stimulus → enthusiasm → movement → new stimulus → detour.**

Cat's POV may narrow.

Dog's often **branches**.

He was going to the park, but then there was the tree, and the tree contained news, and the news led to the hedge, and behind the hedge was his friend, and now the park can wait for three seconds because THIS IS IMPORTANT.

### Lexical tendencies

Dog notices:

- towards;
- there;
- again;
- friend;
- new;
- familiar;
- someone;
- trail;
- food;
- outside;
- go;
- run;
- come;
- together;
- now.

Dog's world is full of invitations.

---

# 23. Things to avoid when writing Dog

- **Do not reduce him to stupidity.** His investigative strategy is rational for his senses.
- **Do not treat every wag as happiness.** Canine tail signals depend on the whole body and context.
- **Do not make him obsessed with dominance or "pack leadership".** Domestic dog relationships are much more flexible than that cliché.
- **Do not make him fearless.** Friendliness can coexist with fear and vulnerability.
- **Do not make smell a superpower with perfect forensic precision.** It is extraordinarily powerful, not magical.
- **Do not make him indifferent to weather simply because he is a dog.** Breed, coat, temperature and personality matter.
- **Do not make him incapable of subtle communication.** Dogs use posture, gaze, ears, tails, scent, vocalisation and touch.
- **Do not make sociability morally superior to Cat's independence.** He misreads her just as seriously as she misreads him.

---

# 24. Opening-chapter lens: Dog on the evening walk

Dog's opening can happen simultaneously with Cat's outdoor chapter but feel like an entirely different world.

The human thinks:

**It's the same evening walk we always take.**

Dog experiences:

- who has already passed along the pavement;
- the possibility that a ball may appear and transform the walk instantly;
- which neighbourhood dogs are outside;
- whether a familiar dog's scent is fresh or old;
- food traces around a bin;
- wet soil and vegetation;
- fox or cat traces;
- wet grass, undergrowth, puddles and mud as places to enter rather than avoid;
- the emotional excitement of familiar dog friends;
- human routines changing as people return home;
- the strange richness of damp evening air.

As the light fades, Dog does not experience Cat's transition into a visually advantageous hunting hour.

Instead, the visible world becomes somewhat less useful while the invisible scent-world continues to pour information towards him.

For this active Springer, the cold and wet are usually background inconveniences beside the extraordinary fact that **every few metres something has happened**.

This lets the two opening chapters describe the same neighbourhood without feeling repetitive.

Cat's evening is made of **sounds, shadows, routes and shelter**.

Dog's is made of **trails, meetings, histories and invitations**.

---

# 25. Dog in one paragraph

Dog is a sturdy, energetic **English Springer Spaniel** who lives in a world of **trails, people, histories, social invitations and things that become understandable when he goes closer**. He is physically robust enough that approach has usually been rewarded rather than punished; his love of movement, chasing and retrieving makes action rewarding in its own right, and his extraordinary sense of smell turns an apparently ordinary street into a constantly updated record of who has been there and what they have done. He considers Cat unnecessarily evasive, moody and difficult to include. His transformation begins when he discovers that the same enthusiastic approach that feels friendly in his own body can feel enormous, loud and dangerous from hers — and that Cat's distance, stillness and caution are not failures to engage with the world but sophisticated ways of understanding it without surrendering safety.

---

# Research grounding

This character file draws particularly on veterinary and behavioural material concerning canine olfaction, scent communication, human-directed social cognition, body-language communication, flexible domestic-dog social organisation and the insulating function of the coat. Useful source families include the **Merck Veterinary Manual**, **VCA Animal Hospitals**, and peer-reviewed canine sensory and behaviour literature.

The story should favour behavioural truth over exaggerated numbers. Dog's nose should feel astonishing because it changes the *structure of his experience*, not because the narration repeatedly announces that it is thousands of times better than a human nose.
