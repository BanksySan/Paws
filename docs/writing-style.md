# Writing Style

## Purpose and scope

This document defines **how the story is told**. It is the working prose and structural guide for writing Cat and Dog's chapters, not a replacement for the creative aims in `docs/vision.md`, the world rules in `docs/canon.md`, the character bibles, or the act and chapter beats.

The goal is to let a reader of around ten years old **inhabit two individual animals' worlds**, experience their misunderstandings, and discover connections between their accounts. The writing should be engaging and clear without becoming simplified, jokey or explanatory.

The rules below express our chosen style. Where a choice remains provisional, it is marked as such. Examples demonstrate technique; **they are not required scenes or plot beats**.

## 1. Point of view and narrative form

1. **Each chapter belongs entirely to Cat or Dog**, identified by the chapter heading: `Cat` or `Dog`. The heading refers to the individual's identity, never the body currently occupied.
2. **First-person narration.** The viewpoint character narrates their own experience as `I`.
3. **Past tense is the working choice (provisional).** It should remain close and immediate, rather than sounding like a wiser narrator looking back after the book's conclusion. Revisit the tense after testing real chapters.
4. **Private, diary-like accounts — not literal diaries.** Chapters should have the intimacy and subjectivity of personal accounts, but neither animal is writing a diary, addressing an audience or deliberately presenting a comprehensive record.
5. **No direct address to the reader.** We inhabit a character's experience; we are not being told a story by a character who knows we are listening.
6. **No external or omniscient viewpoint, even briefly.** If neither animal has witnessed or otherwise become aware of an event, it cannot be shown directly. The reader discovers what is happening through what Cat and Dog encounter, notice, remember or infer.

## 2. Two interlocking accounts

1. **Chapters alternate strictly:** Cat, Dog, Cat, Dog, beginning with Cat in the current plan. Do not change the pattern merely to accommodate a particular scene.
2. **Overlapping viewpoints are a defining feature, not an occasional flourish.** We will often revisit events or stretches of time through the other animal's account. The first two chapters, for example, take place at almost the same time.
3. **Prefer interlocking rather than mechanically parallel coverage.** The chapters need not begin and end together. Cat might narrate an afternoon while Dog's account begins earlier and carries on beyond the event they share.
4. **A shared event need not be equally important to both.** One animal may barely notice what dominates the other's account; there is no requirement to give each event an equal or opposite reaction.
5. **Let the reader make the connections.** Shared places, actions, sensations and consequences should establish chronology naturally. Ordinarily, a heading identifying `Cat` or `Dog` is enough; avoid explanatory labels such as *The same evening* or explicit timestamps. Add signposting only if the narrative itself cannot be made clear.
6. **One underlying reality, different accounts.** Physical events follow a coherent sequence, but Cat and Dog may witness different parts, interpret them differently or remember them imperfectly. Neither perspective is automatically the definitive account.
7. **The reader may understand more than either animal.** Do not insert a narrator's explanation to resolve discrepancies or spell out a misunderstanding. Provide sufficient evidence for a ten-year-old to work it out; mystery should come from perspective, not muddled storytelling.

## 3. Narrative prose and immediate thoughts

The text has **two consistent modes**:

- **Narrative prose:** first-person, normally past tense. It recounts perceptions, actions, experiences, memories and thoughts integrated into the account.
- **Direct, immediate thoughts:** *italicised*. These present a thought as it occurs in the character's mind, in whichever tense or form feels natural — including questions, commands, sudden realisations and exclamations.

Use italics **consistently for direct thoughts**, not just for moments of extreme emotion. This does not mean italicising all mental activity: `I wondered where the noise had come from` is narrative prose; *Where did that come from?* is a directly rendered thought.

For example, from Dog's point of view:

> Big Human went towards the cupboard.
>
> *The lead! We're going out!*
>
> I hurried across the kitchen. Big Human stopped to pick something up from the table.
>
> *Come on. The outside is waiting!*

The contrast between narrative prose and immediate thought should make the reader feel close to the character, **not** create the impression that another animal can hear those thoughts. Avoid italicising a phrase simply for decorative emphasis if it could be mistaken for a direct thought.

## 4. An animal's world, not a human account of it

1. **Experience comes before explanation.** The reader should discover Cat's and Dog's worlds through what captures their attention, what information their senses provide, what they want and what they do.
2. **Sensory description should be rich but selective.** Describe what matters to the individual in that moment; do not produce a poetic inventory of every sight, smell, sound and texture in a scene.
3. **Normal senses should feel normal to their owner.** Dog does not marvel at every smell, nor Cat at every small sound. The reader can find a perception extraordinary while the narrator takes it for granted.
4. **Their attention is not interchangeable with human attention.** A lead, chair, telephone, garden or human routine may be familiar but significant for reasons different from those a human would give.
5. **Ordinary English is a literary convention.** Cat and Dog's private narratives are written in clear, natural English. They can express detailed thoughts, opinions, memories, humour and reasoning; this does not give them human cultural knowledge or a human interpretation of things.
6. **Do not manufacture a contrast.** Cat and Dog are individuals, not opposites. They may share interests, ignore the same event, react similarly or attend to completely different things. Their actions must be credible for them, not chosen to illustrate a point or complete a comic pairing.

The test is not *How can I describe this room as a cat or dog would?* but *What would this particular animal notice here, and why would it matter?*

## 5. Distinctive but unforced voices

Both narrators use expressive British English. Their voices should nevertheless be recognisably individual through:

- what they notice first and what they overlook;
- what they treat as important, threatening, inviting or irrelevant;
- the assumptions they make and how those assumptions shape action;
- the natural rhythm of their attention and thoughts;
- their individual phrasing, sentence lengths, pauses and digressions.

Cat may linger over a quiet movement, a route or a change in her surroundings. Dog may follow a scent, an activity or a social opportunity. **These are possibilities, not mandatory formulas.** Cat need not always be terse; Dog need not always be exuberant. Neither should be given a fixed comic mannerism, reduced intelligence or an exaggerated vocabulary merely to distinguish the prose.

A useful test: without a heading, does the passage feel like the individual? If the bodies have been swapped, can we still recognise the narrator from their interpretation and choices?

## 6. Communication and the human household

### Human speech and identities

- **Filter human speech through the viewpoint animal.** Normally describe the voice, tone, familiar sounds or recognised words that matter to Cat or Dog, rather than transcribing complete human conversations.
- Quote human words only when the animal could reasonably recognise or attend to them, and when the exact wording serves the experience. Full human dialogue must not become a back door to a human narrator's knowledge.
- Use consistent household identifiers such as **Big Human** and **Little Human** for the same people in both accounts. They are known through shared history, scent, voice, habits and routines, not necessarily through human names or family relationships. Do not invent an elaborate second naming scheme for each animal.
- The importance of a human is shown through what the animal expects from and experiences with that person. Dog might notice Big Human approaching the lead and anticipate a walk; Cat might register a familiar warm lap or the prospect of Dog going out.

### Communication between Cat and Dog

- Their communication is **physical, behavioural and imperfect**, as established in `docs/canon.md`: movement, posture, tails, ears, gaze, sound, distance, touch and learnt associations. There is no conversational animal dialogue or telepathy.
- **Render the signals and the narrator's experience of them.** Do not routinely attach a neat, omniscient translation to each gesture.
- The narrator **may absolutely understand and state their own intention**, including in a direct italicised thought: *Why doesn't he get the hint? My tail is flicking. Back off!*
- The narrator may form confident conclusions about the other's signals, including mistaken ones. Such interpretations belong to that character, not to the objective narration.
- Let the reader discover how two sincere interpretations can produce conflict. Familiarity with a few unmistakable warnings does not imply perfect understanding of subtler signals.
- As understanding develops after the swap, show it through changed attention, choices and responses — not through sudden fluency in a shared language.

## 7. Language, description and figurative expression

1. **Write for a capable ten-year-old.** Use natural, rich, readable British English (`en-GB`), varied sentence lengths and interesting vocabulary where context makes its meaning clear. Neither oversimplify nor write elaborately to sound literary.
2. **Figurative language belongs within the viewpoint.** Metaphors and comparisons should generally draw on things Cat or Dog might recognise or experience. Avoid references to human concepts merely to explain an animal's perception to the reader.
3. **Prefer sensory causality to sensory decoration.** A smell leads Dog to investigate; a noise causes Cat to stop and listen. Description earns its place by influencing attention, understanding, atmosphere or action.
4. **Allow calm and stillness.** A chapter need not be busy to be engaging. Waiting, listening, watching and noticing can be meaningful activity to the narrator.
5. **Avoid unnecessary exposition.** We don't have to explain a familiar ability repeatedly. Reserve closer exploration for situations where a sensation becomes surprising, difficult or newly meaningful.

## 8. Emotion, learning and humour

- **Show rather than tell where possible.** Reveal feelings through attention, physical sensations, actions, choices and immediate thoughts rather than routinely naming emotions. Direct declarations such as `I was frightened` are permitted when they feel natural.
- **Let discoveries happen before they are explained.** Understanding should emerge primarily from experience and later behaviour. Cat and Dog may reflect on what happens, but should not deliver psychological analyses or speeches announcing the story's lesson.
- **Humour is incidental, not an objective.** The book is not primarily a comedy. Funny things may arise naturally from encounters, errors, surprises and physical circumstances, but don't bend behaviour to create a punchline.
- **Indifference is allowed.** What delights Dog may not matter to Cat at all, except perhaps because he is getting too close. There is no obligation for an amusing reaction, a judgement or a complementary counter-action.
- **No artificial moral superiority.** Misunderstandings can be painful, frustrating, tender, funny or entirely unremarkable. Neither animal is there to be the clever one, the foolish one or the comic relief.

## 9. The swap as a narrative test

- **Chapter names track identity**, so a `Cat` chapter remains Cat's account when she is in Dog's body. No special title or recurring explanatory reminder is needed.
- **Personality and viewpoint remain with the individual.** Perception, reflexes, timing, bodily impulses and physical possibilities change with the occupied body, in accordance with `docs/canon.md`.
- **Allow the unfamiliar body to influence the prose subtly.** A new sensation can interrupt attention; unexpected strength can change what seems possible; a rapid reflex can surprise the narrator. This should emerge organically, not become a new personality or an imposed sentence pattern.
- **Discoveries are earned.** Cat may enjoy powerful running without suddenly acquiring Dog's preferences or skills. Dog may experience feline stillness, fast reactions and stealth without instantly becoming an expert hunter.
- **Do not narrate the moral.** The reader should recognise growing understanding in the animals' experiences and choices. A character may have a direct realisation, but should not be obliged to articulate the theme.
- **Physical reminders must serve the character.** Mention a heavy paw, sensitive whisker or unfamiliar tail when it genuinely draws attention, not merely to reassure the reader which body is being occupied.

## 10. Chapter pacing, danger and endings

1. **Flexible length.** Let a chapter be as long as its account needs, with a general bias towards manageable chapters rather than a fixed word count.
2. **Natural compression of time.** Skip uneventful periods and linger where the individual is absorbed, unsettled, learning or in danger. A brief stretch of chronological time may demand many paragraphs; an unimportant afternoon may pass in a sentence.
3. **Real jeopardy.** Danger, fear, injury and uncertainty may be genuine. Do not soften them into harmless adventure merely because the audience is young; equally, avoid gratuitous or graphic violence.
4. **Keep fear within the viewpoint.** A danger is felt through what the animal perceives and can or cannot do, rather than through warnings from an omniscient narrator.
5. **Natural stopping points.** End a chapter where that account has reached a meaningful pause or conclusion. Quiet endings are as legitimate as suspenseful ones. Cliffhangers should arise from events, not be imposed as a routine.

## 11. Drafting and revision checks

When reviewing a chapter, ask:

1. Can every observation and piece of knowledge be traced to the viewpoint character's experience, memory or reasonable inference?
2. Does the chapter immerse us in **this individual animal's** concerns, rather than describing an animal from outside?
3. Are direct immediate thoughts italicised consistently, while ordinary narrative prose remains readable?
4. Does any human speech reveal information the animal would not plausibly attend to or understand?
5. Does animal communication appear as behaviour and subjective interpretation, rather than translated conversation?
6. If the chapter overlaps another, can a reader recognise the shared events without an explanatory timestamp or omniscient correction?
7. Is a reaction genuine to the character, rather than forced for contrast, humour, a lesson or a plot convenience?
8. Are emotion and discovery experienced before they are explained?
9. During the swap, is the **individual** recognisable while the **body's** sensations and responses remain convincing?
10. Is the prose vivid, clear and appropriate for a ten-year-old, without being patronising?

## Working decisions to test in manuscript

- **First-person past tense:** chosen provisionally; test whether it sustains immediacy across several chapters.
- **Diary-like presentation without literal diaries:** preferred; consider more explicit temporal markers only if readers genuinely struggle to follow the overlapping accounts.
- **Narrator-only headings:** strongly preferred. First improve the prose's orientation cues before adding dates or times.

Other conventions above reflect decisions reached during the writing-style discussion. Adjust them deliberately if manuscript testing reveals a problem, rather than allowing individual chapters to drift into different rules.
