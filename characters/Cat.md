# Character File: Cat

## Purpose of this file

This is an **author-facing character bible** for Cat: not simply a list of feline facts, but a guide to what the world may *feel like* from inside Cat's body and temperament. It should help keep her point of view consistent before and after the body swap.

The guiding principle is:

> **Cat does not behave like a small, aloof dog. Her behaviour makes sense because she inhabits a different body, receives different information, and manages danger differently.**

Cat is a **small-ish tortoiseshell domestic shorthair — an ordinary moggie**. She is light, agile and physically precise, with a short coat and a strong visual identity without belonging to a specialised pedigree breed.

Her tortoiseshell colouring should not be used as a shortcut to personality. The factual basis should guide the fiction rather than imprison it; individual cats vary enormously, and Cat is one particular cat.

---

# 1. Core character idea

Cat is **small, observant, quick, self-contained and cautious**. She survives by knowing what is happening *before* she commits herself.

Her confidence is not Dog's confidence. Dog can often afford to enter a situation and discover what it is once he gets there. Cat would rather understand the space first.

Her basic strategy is:

**Observe → locate → assess → choose → move.**

Dog's behaviour can therefore seem appallingly reckless to her. He blunders into rooms, runs towards noises, pushes his nose into things, announces himself loudly and assumes strangers are potential friends.

To Cat, this is not bravery. It is a bewildering lack of self-preservation.

---

# 2. Cat's sensory world

## Sensory hierarchy

For the purposes of the story, Cat's world can be written primarily through:

1. **Hearing** — where is it, how small is it, is it moving?
2. **Sight and movement** — what changed, what crossed the edge of vision?
3. **Touch and air movement** — whiskers, paws, fur, surfaces, draughts.
4. **Smell** — important for familiarity, territory and social information, but not the overwhelming narrative map that smell can be for Dog.
5. **Taste** — close to smell, selective, texture-conscious.

This does **not** mean Cat has poor smell. She doesn't. The distinction is narrative: Dog can be written as living inside a landscape made of scent; Cat can be written as living inside a landscape of **sound, movement, edges, heights and cover**.

---

## Hearing: a world full of tiny events

Cat hears things humans routinely fail to notice and can detect much higher-frequency sounds than we can.

This changes the apparent scale of events.

A human hears: *a quiet garden.*

Cat may hear:

- paws or claws moving through leaves;
- a mouse shifting beneath vegetation;
- wings fluttering in a hedge;
- the scratch of something behind a fence;
- rain ticking differently on leaf, soil, stone and metal;
- a door opening two rooms away;
- Dog's collar moving before Dog himself appears;
- the minute change in Dog's breathing when he becomes excited.

Cat's attention may suddenly snap towards something the human reader has not yet been told exists.

### Writing consequence

Cat does not need to *see* something before it matters.

A useful Cat sentence often begins with an interruption:

> Something moved beneath the hedge.

Or:

> There — again. A tiny dry scratching underneath the wet leaves.

She can notice the event first and identify its source later.

---

## Sight: excellent in dim light, tuned for movement

Cat's vision is especially useful around dusk and in low light. She cannot literally see in total darkness, but she can function at light levels at which a human would feel almost blind.

For Cat, dusk is therefore not simply the world disappearing.

It is **the world changing**.

Bright colour and fine daytime detail become less important. Movement, outline, contrast, gleam and shadow become more important.

The evening garden may feel more alive as it becomes less visually accessible to the humans.

### What Cat looks for

- movement against stillness;
- silhouettes;
- the gap beneath a gate;
- the shadow under a parked car;
- branches and ledges that form routes;
- open ground that must be crossed;
- places from which another animal could appear;
- reflective eyes;
- the safest vertical route.

### Narrative implication

Humans tend to describe a room by its contents.

Cat may describe it by its **routes**:

- floor to chair;
- chair to table;
- table to windowsill;
- windowsill to curtain rail;
- underneath the sofa;
- behind the door;
- up, across, down, out.

A room is not a collection of furniture. It is a three-dimensional network of escape routes, ambush points, lookout posts and warm surfaces.

---

## Whiskers and close-range perception

Cat's whiskers are sensory structures, not decoration. They provide information about nearby objects, air movement and close spaces.

This gives Cat another layer of spatial awareness at very close range, precisely where eyesight is less useful.

### Writing possibilities

Cat may feel:

- the narrowness of a gap before her shoulders enter it;
- a nearby object without looking directly at it;
- a faint movement of air caused by something moving close by;
- the edge of a surface beneath her face in darkness.

Dog-in-Cat may initially find this extraordinary: the face itself seems to be *touching the room before the body gets there*.

---

## Smell: identity, familiarity and disturbance

Cat's sense of smell is far better than a human's and is deeply important to her sense of familiarity and social comfort.

The house has a smell that means **home**.

Objects carry identities. People carry identities. Cat herself leaves scent as she rubs against familiar surfaces and people.

An unfamiliar smell can therefore represent more than an interesting fact. It can mean:

**Something has changed inside my territory.**

Where Dog may be attracted towards a strange smell to investigate it, Cat may initially treat an unfamiliar smell as a reason for caution.

---

# 3. Cat's body

## Smallness is not merely size

Cat is a small-ish moggie, light even by comparison with Dog's sturdy Springer body. The human world is physically different for her.

A human foot is large.

A child running down the hallway is an approaching mass.

A closing door is not a mild inconvenience.

Dog does not merely look large: a sturdy adult Springer occupies an enormous amount of Cat's immediate visual and physical world.

When he rushes towards her, even playfully, Cat has little reason to assume he will stop with perfect precision.

### The crucial idea

Cat's caution is partly rational because **the consequences of being wrong are greater for her**.

Dog can collide with Cat accidentally and hurt her.

Cat cannot collide with Dog with equivalent consequences.

This asymmetry should sit underneath many of their early misunderstandings.

---

## Lightness

Cat's body allows extraordinary acceleration, jumping and changes of direction.

She does not move like a scaled-down dog.

Her movement may alternate between:

- absolute stillness;
- slow, economical placement;
- sudden explosive speed;
- immediate stillness again.

This rhythm is useful in prose.

Dog often **continues**.

Cat often **pauses**.

Dog bounds.

Cat places.

Dog carries momentum.

Cat redirects momentum.

---

## Balance and verticality

Height is part of Cat's normal world.

A high place provides:

- safety;
- visibility;
- distance from Dog;
- time to decide;
- control over who can approach.

Humans may think Cat is hiding on top of a wardrobe.

Cat may think she is finally standing somewhere sensible.

### Important emotional distinction

Retreating upwards is not necessarily defeat.

For Cat, distance can be **control**.

---

## Fragility and confidence

Cat should not think of herself as fragile all day long. Animals generally inhabit their bodies as normal.

She feels **capable**.

She climbs things Dog cannot climb, fits through gaps he cannot use, falls distances that would alarm him, changes direction faster than he can, and disappears almost at will.

Her vulnerability becomes salient when something large corners, grabs, blocks or strikes her.

This distinction will matter enormously after the swap.

Dog-in-Cat may initially feel weak because he compares Cat's body with his old one.

Cat never thought of it as weak.

To Cat it was **light, precise and fast**.

---

# 4. Space and territory

Cat's home is not merely where her humans live. It is a known map of safe places, resources, smells, routes and boundaries.

She pays attention to:

- which doors are open;
- who is in which room;
- whether Dog is asleep;
- whether the children are running around;
- whether a familiar chair has moved;
- whether something unfamiliar has appeared;
- which window is warm;
- which route outdoors is available.

Cats often manage social situations by **distance**. This is extremely useful for the book.

Where Dog may solve uncertainty by approaching, Cat often solves uncertainty by increasing distance until she has enough information.

That means Dog can unintentionally make every disagreement worse by following her.

Cat withdraws.

Dog thinks: *She wants me to chase her!* or *Where's she going?*

Dog follows.

Cat thinks: *He ignored the warning.*

She withdraws further.

Dog follows again.

Eventually Cat hisses or swats.

Dog is astonished that she has become aggressive "for no reason".

From Cat's point of view, she has issued a long sequence of perfectly clear requests.

---

# 5. Cat outdoors

Cat is not "going for a walk".

Her outdoor journey does not need a destination.

It can consist of a sequence of investigations, observations and decisions.

She may:

- leave the house and immediately stop;
- listen before proceeding;
- patrol familiar routes;
- check scent marks;
- inspect changes since her last visit;
- sit beneath cover and watch;
- climb a fence for a better view;
- investigate a rustle;
- stalk something;
- abandon the stalk instantly when conditions change;
- wait for another cat to pass;
- cross open ground quickly;
- spend ten minutes apparently doing nothing;
- return by a completely different route.

The important point is that **stillness is activity**.

Cat sitting motionless beneath a hedge can be processing a vast amount of information.

A human child might say, "She's just sitting there."

Cat would regard that as an extraordinary misunderstanding of what is happening.

---

# 6. Weather

## Rain

Rain changes Cat's world in several ways at once.

Cat's short coat is not an excuse to treat her as incapable of coping outdoors, but when it becomes soaked the wet is immediate. It makes fur wet, heavy and less comfortable. Wet fur can reduce insulation, takes time to dry and may make movement feel less free.

But rain also changes the **information environment**:

- surfaces sound different;
- smells change;
- moving water creates noise;
- vegetation bends and drips;
- familiar dry routes may become unpleasant;
- sheltered places become more valuable.

Cat is therefore not merely thinking, *I hate being wet.*

Rain has altered the entire map.

Dog splashing delightedly through a puddle is incomprehensible.

---

## Cold

Cat is small and lightly built compared with Dog's sturdy Springer body. Warmth matters.

Warm places become resources:

- a radiator;
- a human lap;
- a blanket;
- a patch of sunlight;
- a warm car bonnet;
- the spot Dog has just vacated.

A sunbeam is not just aesthetically pleasant.

It is a place where Cat can stop spending energy keeping herself warm.

This gives warmth an almost luxurious quality.

---

## Snow

Snow may be beautiful to Cat's human family and thrilling to Dog, but it can transform Cat's familiar outdoor world into something physically awkward and informationally strange.

Her paws meet cold, wet material. Familiar scent and surface cues are altered. The ground no longer feels like itself.

Cat may therefore stand at the open door, put out one paw, and reach a perfectly reasonable conclusion:

**No.**

---

# 7. Hunting and play

Cat is a solitary hunter by ancestry and behaviour. Hunting involves a sequence rather than generic "playfulness":

**detect → orient → watch → stalk → chase → pounce → seize**.

A dangling string can activate parts of this system.

So can a leaf scraping across paving stones.

So can something tiny moving under a cupboard.

### Why this matters for character

Cat values:

- patience;
- timing;
- concealment;
- precision;
- the ability to stop moving.

These are almost the inverse of Dog's strengths.

During the climax, these should become indispensable rather than merely cute feline traits.

---

# 8. Social life

Cat should **not** be written as fundamentally antisocial.

Cats have flexible social systems. They can form strong relationships with cats, humans and other animals, but they often regulate interaction more carefully than dogs do.

Cat's preferred relationship may involve:

- being in the same room without touching;
- approaching when she chooses;
- rubbing against someone;
- sitting beside rather than on them;
- slow blinking;
- grooming;
- sleeping near someone trusted;
- sharing space while both parties remain quiet.

Dog may fail to recognise any of this as affection because his idea of friendship is more overt.

Cat may spend an entire evening six feet from a human and consider that intimate companionship.

Dog may think she is ignoring everybody.

---

# 9. Humans

Cat understands humans extremely well in some contexts and finds them baffling in others.

Humans are:

- enormous;
- warm;
- useful door-openers;
- food providers;
- makers of comfortable furniture;
- sometimes affectionate;
- sometimes noisy;
- astonishingly poor at noticing small sounds;
- prone to touching Cat when Cat has not requested touching;
- strangely unable to understand that the closed door is a serious error requiring immediate correction.

Cat may love the family deeply without behaving like Dog.

This is important for children reading the story: **different expressions of affection can represent equally real affection**.

---

# 10. Communication

Cat communicates with her whole body.

Important signals include:

- ear orientation;
- pupil size;
- head position;
- body tension;
- tail height and motion;
- fur position;
- whether she approaches or withdraws;
- vocalisation;
- touch;
- deliberate distance.

## The tail misunderstanding

This is one of the strongest recurring mechanisms in the book.

Dog sees a moving tail and maps it onto canine experience: **arousal, excitement, perhaps invitation**.

But a cat sharply flicking or lashing her tail may be increasingly irritated or overstimulated.

So Dog sees:

**movement → enthusiasm → continue!**

Cat means:

**movement → warning → stop!**

This can escalate naturally without either character being stupid.

---

## Hissing

A hiss should often be understood as **distance-making**, not villainy or an eagerness to fight.

Cat would usually rather the threat stop approaching than have to attack it.

The hiss says, in effect:

**You have come too close. Do not come closer.**

Dog may initially hear only hostility.

After inhabiting Cat's body, he may recognise it as the final warning produced by an animal that has already run out of easier ways to create space.

---

# 11. Danger

Cat's preferred danger sequence is roughly:

**detect early → avoid → hide or escape → warn if approached → fight if necessary**.

Her great defence is that the dangerous event never quite reaches her.

This can look like cowardice to Dog.

But Cat might look at Dog's strategy — run towards it barking — and regard him as a lunatic.

### When cornered

The emotional shift should be dramatic.

A cat with escape routes is cautious.

A cat without escape routes may become explosively violent.

That makes the burglary sequence particularly powerful whichever structural version we ultimately choose.

### If the burglary happens after the return

Cat can deliberately use her own feline strengths in defence of Dog: darkness, cover, height, speed, claws and immediate retreat. A sudden attack from the shadows can be a conscious tactical choice rather than evidence that she is inherently aggressive.

### If the burglary happens while they are still swapped

Dog-in-Cat may have to perform those actions instead. Cat's role then becomes partly that of expert and coach: she knows what this small body can survive, how far it can jump, where it can hide and when it must not confront a human head-on.

In either version, feline violence should remain **brief, tactical and distance-making**, not prolonged combat.

---

# 12. Emotional logic

Cat's deepest needs are likely to include:

- **control over proximity**;
- **predictability**;
- **safe retreat**;
- **knowledge of her environment**;
- **freedom of movement**;
- **warmth**;
- **quiet companionship on mutually understood terms**.

Her strongest negative experiences include:

- being trapped;
- being pursued;
- being grabbed unexpectedly;
- sudden loud noises nearby;
- unfamiliar intrusion into familiar space;
- having no high or hidden place available;
- Dog ignoring increasingly strong signals to stop.

Her arc is **not** learning that caution is wrong.

It is learning that Dog's openness, sociability and willingness to commit can sometimes achieve things observation cannot.

---

# 13. Cat's view of Dog before the swap

Dog is:

- far too large;
- far too loud;
- always in the way;
- incapable of entering a room normally;
- obsessed with smelling disgusting things;
- willing to chase the same ball repeatedly even after successfully returning it;
- bizarrely delighted by strangers;
- unable to recognise a warning;
- physically careless;
- embarrassingly needy around humans;
- occasionally useful as a warm object;
- probably not malicious;
- nevertheless exhausting.

The important qualifier is **probably not malicious**.

Cat need not hate Dog. She may simply believe that living with him is like sharing a house with a cheerful minor natural disaster.

---

# 14. Cat's hidden misunderstanding

Cat assumes Dog experiences the world more or less as she does, but behaves differently by choice.

Therefore:

- if he makes noise, he must not care how much noise matters;
- if he runs into a space without surveying it, he must be foolish;
- if he approaches strangers, he must be reckless;
- if he gets wet, he must simply have terrible standards;
- if he keeps retrieving a ball the humans repeatedly throw away, he must have misunderstood the purpose of the exercise;
- if he follows her when she retreats, he must be deliberately ignoring her.

The body swap reveals the flaw:

**Dog is not receiving the same world and responding badly to it. He is receiving a different world.**

---

# 15. Cat inside Dog's body

This is where Cat's assumptions fail.

## First shocks

### Size

The floor is farther away.

Her Springer paws are enormous.

She cannot disappear through the old gaps.

She turns and the rest of Dog's sturdy body seems to keep coming.

Furniture that once formed routes now forms barriers.

But humans seem suddenly smaller.

Dog's physical confidence begins to make sense. So does some of his apparent clumsiness: this body carries weight and momentum Cat has never had to manage.

### Smell

This should be one of Cat's great revelations.

Smell is no longer an attribute of objects.

It becomes **structure**.

The hallway contains histories.

The garden contains routes she cannot see.

A lamppost is an information board.

A human's trousers contain news from somewhere else.

The wind carries the presence of things long before they appear.

Cat may find it overwhelming, vulgar and astonishing — and then addictive.

### Social gravity

Dog's body seems drawn towards people and dogs.

Part of this is temperament, but part is that approaching gives access to richer information.

Cat finally understands the mechanic:

**to smell properly, Dog often has to go closer.**

Her own senses allow her to learn while keeping distance.

His most informative sense rewards proximity.

This may be the single most important insight in the book.

### Chasing and retrieving

A thrown ball may produce another revelation.

Cat has always regarded Dog's willingness to retrieve an object only for the humans to throw it away again as evidence of questionable judgement.

Inside Dog's body, movement can feel like an invitation before she has had time to formulate an opinion. The chase uses the whole body: eyes, legs, balance, mouth, momentum, anticipation.

She does not have to become a ball-obsessed dog mentally. It is enough for her to discover why the activity can feel extraordinarily rewarding from inside this body.

### Strength

For the first time Cat can push rather than evade.

A closed door, a heavy object, another animal: physical force is available as an option.

She may initially overuse it because it feels extraordinary.

Then she learns that a large body creates responsibility. Other creatures flinch when she moves quickly. A turn, greeting or collision that would be trivial in her own body can now move objects — or people.

---

# 16. Cat's post-swap growth

Cat does not become dog-like.

She retains:

- caution;
- precision;
- love of warmth;
- desire for space;
- quiet observation;
- scepticism towards unnecessary wetness.

But she now understands:

- why Dog goes close to things;
- why smell is information rather than filth;
- why Dog sometimes commits before she thinks he should;
- why humans and other dogs are genuinely rewarding to him;
- why his body encourages a different kind of confidence;
- why his noise can be protective rather than merely disruptive;
- why chasing and retrieving can be intrinsically satisfying rather than evidence that he has failed to notice the humans keep throwing the ball away.

She may even discover that she misses being able to smell the whole street.

---

# 17. Voice guide for Cat POV

Cat's prose should tend towards:

- specificity;
- economy;
- spatial awareness;
- interrupted attention;
- short bursts of action;
- noticing small changes;
- understated humour;
- strong opinions that she considers obvious facts.

She probably does not narrate herself as anxious.

She narrates the world as full of things that require sensible assessment.

### Useful rhythm

**Stillness → sensory cue → assessment → precise action.**

### Lexical tendencies

Cat notices:

- beneath;
- above;
- behind;
- edge;
- gap;
- shadow;
- scrape;
- twitch;
- click;
- rustle;
- warm;
- damp;
- exposed;
- safe;
- near;
- too near.

She is less likely than Dog to describe a place as a broad emotional whole. She notices the *one thing that has changed*.

---

# 18. Things to avoid when writing Cat

- **Do not make her cold because she is a cat.** She can be deeply affectionate.
- **Do not make every hiss an attack.** It is often an attempt to prevent one.
- **Do not make caution equal cowardice.** It is an adaptive strategy.
- **Do not make her sense of smell negligible.** The contrast with Dog is relative, not absolute.
- **Do not make her perfectly solitary.** Cats have flexible social relationships.
- **Do not make her magic.** She cannot see in total darkness.
- **Do not have her constantly conscious of being fragile.** Her normal experience of her own body is competence.
- **Do not turn feline behaviour into moral superiority.** Cat misreads Dog as badly as Dog misreads Cat.

---

# 19. Opening-chapter lens: Cat at dusk

A strong Cat opening can exploit the transition from daylight to night.

The humans might experience:

**It's getting dark, cold and wet. Time to go home.**

Cat experiences a more complicated transformation:

- human activity is diminishing;
- tiny animal activity is becoming more detectable;
- shadows deepen but are not yet disabling;
- headlights and windows become bright islands;
- wet surfaces change sound;
- the cold begins to matter;
- sheltered routes become preferable;
- the garden becomes simultaneously more interesting and less comfortable.

This gives the opening a useful contradiction:

**Cat is sensory-equipped for the coming darkness but physically unimpressed by the coming cold and rain.**

She can be drawn onwards by sound while every wet paw argues for home.

---

# 20. Cat in one paragraph

Cat is a **small-ish tortoiseshell moggie** who lives in a world of **small sounds, sudden movements, known routes, safe heights and carefully controlled distances**. She is physically light and extraordinarily capable, but a mistake involving a much larger animal can hurt her, so she prefers to know before she commits. She is social on selective terms, affectionate without being demonstrative, and intensely aware of changes in familiar territory. She considers Dog loud, reckless, invasive and absurdly interested in smells. Her transformation begins when she discovers that Dog's proximity, noise and confidence are not defective versions of her own strategies: they arise from a body and sensory world in which getting close provides information, size provides safety, and meeting the unknown can genuinely feel exciting.

---

# Research grounding

This character file draws particularly on veterinary and behavioural material concerning feline low-light vision, high-frequency hearing, whisker function, solitary hunting, flexible social organisation, vertical safe spaces, olfactory communication and distance-regulating behaviour. Useful source families include the **Merck Veterinary Manual**, **Cornell Feline Health Center**, **VCA Animal Hospitals**, and peer-reviewed feline behaviour literature.

The story should favour behavioural truth over rigid numerical claims: individual cats vary, and the aim is to make Cat's viewpoint *plausible and internally coherent*, not to make her a zoology textbook.
